Panic disorder symptoms, causes & treatment

A panic attack can feel sudden, intense, and frightening. Many people describe it as a wave of fear that comes out of nowhere, even when there is no real danger around them. During a panic attack, the body reacts as if it is facing a serious threat, which can cause strong physical and emotional symptoms.

For some people, a panic disorder may feel like a heart attack, choking, fainting, or losing control. Although panic attacks are usually not physically dangerous, they can be very distressing and may affect daily life if they happen often. In this guide we will give an overview about this problem.

How does a panic attack feel?

A panic attack often feels like your body is in danger, even when you are safe. The feeling can build quickly and may reach its strongest point within minutes. Many people feel exhausted, emotional, or worried after a panic attack. Some may also become afraid of having another attack, which can make anxiety worse over time.

  • Sudden intense fear or panic
  • Racing heartbeat or pounding chest
  • Shortness of breath or feeling unable to breathe
  • Chest tightness or chest pain
  • Dizziness, shaking, or feeling faint
  • Sweating, chills, or hot flushes
  • Nausea or stomach discomfort
  • Tingling or numbness in hands, face, or body
  • Feeling detached from reality or your surroundings
  • Fear of dying, losing control, or going crazy

What causes panic attacks?

A panic attack can happen for many different reasons. Sometimes there is a clear trigger, while other times it may happen without warning. The exact cause is not always clear, but panic attacks are often linked to the body’s fight-or-flight response becoming overactive.

  • Stressful life events: Work pressure, family problems, grief, or trauma can increase anxiety.
  • Panic disorder: Repeated panic attacks may happen as part of panic disorder.
  • Family history: People with close family members who experience panic may have a higher risk.
  • Brain chemical imbalance: Changes in brain chemicals may affect anxiety and panic response.
  • Phobias: Fear of certain places, situations, or objects can trigger panic.
  • Health worries: Fear about physical symptoms may increase panic.
  • Caffeine, alcohol, or drugs: Some substances can increase heart rate and anxiety.
  • Poor sleep: Lack of proper sleep can make the nervous system more sensitive.
  • Medical conditions: Thyroid problems, heart rhythm issues, or breathing conditions may cause similar symptoms.

Common symptoms of panic attacks

Panic attack symptoms can affect both the body and mind. The symptoms may feel severe, but they usually pass after a short time. Because panic attack symptoms can look similar to serious medical problems, medical advice is important if symptoms are new, severe, or unusual. Here are some common symptoms:

  • Fast heartbeat: Your heart may feel like it is racing, skipping, or pounding.
  • Breathing difficulty: You may feel like you cannot get enough air.
  • Chest discomfort: Tightness or pain in the chest can feel frightening.
  • Shaking or trembling: Your hands, legs, or whole body may shake.
  • Sweating: You may suddenly sweat even in a cool environment.
  • Dizziness: You may feel lightheaded, weak, or unsteady.
  • Fear of danger: You may feel something terrible is about to happen.
  • Numbness or tingling: This can happen in the fingers, lips, face, or feet.
  • Feeling unreal: Some people feel disconnected from themselves or the place around them.

What to do during a panic attack

The main goal during a panic attack is to remind your body and mind that you are safe.
Simple steps can help reduce fear and allow the attack to pass naturally.

  • Focus on slow breathing: Breathe in gently through your nose and breathe out slowly.
  • Remind yourself it will pass: Say, “This is a panic attack. It is uncomfortable, but it will pass.”
  • Stay where you are if safe: Try not to run away immediately, as avoidance can make fear stronger.
  • Use grounding: Look around and name things you can see, hear, feel, and touch.
  • Relax your muscles: Slowly loosen your shoulders, hands, jaw, and legs.
  • Avoid fighting the feeling: Accepting the panic can reduce the fear cycle.
  • Sip water: This may help you feel more present and steady.
  • Move gently: Slow walking or stretching may help release tension.

When should you get medical help?

Panic attacks can be managed, but professional support is important if they continue. You should not ignore repeated attacks, especially if they affect your work, sleep, travel, or relationships.

  • You have panic attacks often.
  • You avoid places because you fear another attack.
  • You feel anxious most of the day.
  • You have chest pain, fainting, or severe breathing problems.
  • Your symptoms are new or different from usual.
  • Panic attacks affect your sleep or daily routine.
  • You use alcohol or substances to cope.
  • You feel hopeless or unsafe.

Treatment options for panic attacks

Panic attacks are treatable with the right support. Treatment depends on your symptoms, medical history, and how often the attacks happen. Medication should only be used as advised by a qualified healthcare professional, especially because some anxiety medicines may cause drowsiness, dependence, or side effects.

  • Talking therapy: Cognitive behavioral therapy can help you understand panic triggers and reduce fear.
  • Lifestyle changes: Better sleep, reduced caffeine, regular meals, and exercise may support recovery.
  • Stress management: Breathing exercises, journaling, prayer, mindfulness, or relaxation can help.
  • Medication: A doctor may prescribe medicines for anxiety or panic symptoms when needed.
  • Regular follow-up: Monitoring symptoms helps adjust treatment safely.
  • Trigger awareness: Learning your patterns can help prevent future attacks.
  • Support network: Talking to trusted people can reduce fear and isolation.

FAQ about panic attacks

What is the main cause of panic attacks?

The main cause is not always clear. Panic attacks may be linked to stress, trauma, family history, anxiety disorders, phobias, poor sleep, or changes in brain chemicals.

Can panic attacks feel like a heart attack?

Yes, panic attacks can cause chest pain, fast heartbeat, sweating, and breathing difficulty, which may feel like a heart attack. If symptoms are new, severe, or unusual, seek urgent medical help.

How long does a panic attack last?

A panic attack often reaches its strongest point within minutes. Many attacks pass within 10 to 30 minutes, but some symptoms may leave you feeling tired or anxious afterwards.

What should I do first during a panic attack?

Start by slowing your breathing and reminding yourself that the attack will pass. Try grounding yourself by focusing on things you can see, hear, touch, and feel.

Can panic attacks be cured?

Many people improve with therapy, lifestyle changes, stress management, and sometimes medication. Some may still have attacks, but treatment can reduce how often they happen and how intense they feel.

Are panic attacks dangerous?

Panic attacks are usually not physically dangerous, but they can feel extremely frightening. Medical advice is important if symptoms are severe, repeated, or similar to heart or breathing problems.

What medicine is used for panic attacks?

Doctors may prescribe different types of medicines depending on the person’s condition, symptoms, and medical history. Never start, stop, or change panic attack medicine without professional guidance.
